What changed, in four charts

Read in order. The first three rule things out; the last one is what is left.

1 It is not that cohorts decay faster than they used to. Line the intakes up by age and the three years sit within a few points of each other. A customer won in 2026 leaves at about the rate one won in 2024 did.

2 It is not that thin months are poisoning the base. Adding the starting months one at a time, the recent ones land inside the same cloud as the early ones. Nothing about the relationship moved across the two years.

3 And it is not hiding at a longer horizon. Give churn one month to happen or six and the cloud lifts without tilting. However long you wait, months with fewer arrivals do not churn more.

4 What changed is the price of a customer. Cut every cohort at the same age so none of the slope is the calendar. What a customer returns has barely moved. What one costs has roughly doubled, and that is the whole of it.

Move the slider on the last chart. The gap holds at every age both halves can reach, which is what rules out the calendar as the explanation. Every figure here is drawn from the same push as the rest of the page.

Forward churn

A cohort curve follows one intake as it decays. These follow the whole base from a standing start: take everyone active in a month, and see how many are still there some months later. That is the number that moves revenue, and it can be asked of every month rather than only of new arrivals.

What was decided, and what is still estimated

Three allocations used to be adjustable here. They are settled now, and applied in the pipeline rather than on this page, so the numbers above arrive already carrying them.

Customer Success
0% of acquisition cost. It is retention work, including its commissions, which are paid on retention. Counting it as acquisition overstated acquisition cost by across the period, of it.
Partnerships
100% of acquisition cost. It sources customers.
Technical Account Manager
Cost of sales, not acquisition. , booked to cost of sales and excluded from every acquisition figure on this page.

There is no control for these because deciding was the point. A slider invites a reader to move a number until they like the answer. The arithmetic is still parameterised in the code, so a different decision is a one line change rather than a rebuild.

What acquisition costs

Every line the pipeline counts as acquisition, by month, with the logo count and the cost per logo underneath it. Partnerships is the one split line that reaches this total, at 100%; Customer Success is settled at 0% and Technical Account Manager sits in cost of sales, so neither appears.

What is solid and what is not

The logo counts, the flows and the retention curves come straight from the workbook and hold whatever anyone decides about cost allocation. The ratios do not. LTV:CAC and payback both move further on the Customer Success split than on every other correction combined, and that split is an open question rather than a measurement.

Two limits worth carrying. Gross profit here is what a cohort has actually returned to date rather than a projection, so young cohorts are understated by construction. And departures are counted two ways: the push books a churn when it sees the transition, while a customer whose subscription drops out of the Stripe export simply stops appearing. The second count is about two thirds higher, and which one you use decides whether the base is growing or shrinking.
