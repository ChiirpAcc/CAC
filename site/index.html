Give churn one month to happen or six and the cloud lifts without tilting. At every horizon the correlation fails to clear zero, and the sign flips across the range rather than holding a direction, which is what noise looks like.

What this means

The fear that a quiet pipeline distracts the team into losing accounts is not supported at any point in two years. Acquisition and retention can be run as separate problems by separate owners, and neither one is hostage to the other. It also means the churn we are about to look at has to be explained by something other than how many customers came in that month.

Worth saying plainly because it will come up: the intake has not in fact been thin. New customers ran 52, 43, 51 and 40 over the last four full months against a two year average of 48.

2 And it is not that cohorts decay faster than they used to, until you get to 2026. Line every intake up against its own signup month. 2024 and 2025 sit almost on top of each other, 79.3% and 78.3% still there at month 6. 2026 is at 67.4%, eleven points below either, and it starts badly rather than ending badly: it loses 9.5% in its first month where the other two lose 2.2% and 3.0%.

What this means

Two years of intakes behaved the same way and the newest one does not. That is not a slow drift, it is a break, and it is concentrated in the first month a customer is with us rather than spread across the year. A cohort that loses a tenth of itself before the second invoice is a problem with what we sold or who we sold it to, not with how we looked after them nine months later.

Read the count carefully, though. It cannot tell a customer who is paying from one who is merely still listed, and going into 2026 both the cancellation rule and the coupons added to the second group. The money version of this same chart is one tab away and reads worse again.

Forward churn

A cohort curve follows one intake as it decays. These follow the whole base from a standing start: take everyone active in a month, and see how many are still there some months later. That is the number that moves revenue, and it can be asked of every month rather than only of new arrivals.

What is solid and what is not

The logo counts, the flows and the retention curves come straight from the workbook and hold whatever anyone decides about cost allocation. The ratios do not. LTV:CAC and payback both move further on the Customer Success split than on every other correction combined, and that split is an open question rather than a measurement.

One limit worth carrying: gross profit here is what a cohort has actually returned to date rather than a projection, so young cohorts are understated by construction.

Departures are counted two ways, and this page uses one of them on purpose. The push books a churn when it sees the transition; a customer whose subscription drops out of the Stripe export simply stops appearing and never generates one. Counting everyone who stops appearing finds about two thirds more. That is not an open question between two defensible numbers, because only one of them reconciles: over 2026 the booked count gives 311 out against 353 in, which would be a base growing by 42, while the base actually fell from 1,160 to 1,048. Counting everyone who stops appearing gives 493 out against 381 in, a fall of 112, which is exactly what happened. So the larger count is the rate and the booked one is a floor, and every churn figure on this page is the larger count unless it says otherwise.
