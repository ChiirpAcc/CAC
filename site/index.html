What changed, in three charts

Read in order. Each one rules something out, and what is left is the pricing question at the bottom.

1 It is not that cohorts decay faster than they used to. But read the shape, not just the endpoint. Line the intakes up by age and at month 6 the three years sit within 3.3 points of each other: 2024 79.3%, 2025 82.2%, 2026 78.8%. The 2026 line arrives there by a different route. It loses nothing at all in its first two months and then 8.4% and 10% in months 5 and 6, the steepest any era has posted at those ages. That is what requiring a customer to serve out a billing period looks like: the leaving is deferred, not prevented. It rests on three cohorts, so the right hand end will move.

What this means

Expect the head count reading in the room: our newest customers are staying longer, so churn must be someone else's problem. At month 6 that is true, and it is not what the chart says. A count cannot tell a customer who is paying from one who is merely still listed, and going into 2026 both the cancellation rule and the coupons added to the second group.

What this chart rules out is a change in how a cohort ages. It does not rule out a change in the conditions every cohort is ageing in, and there has been one. Measured by calendar month rather than by cohort age, churn among customers under six months old has more than doubled in the past year, from 4.1% a month to 9.5%, and churn among everyone older has more than doubled too, from 2.5% to 6.1%. The deterioration is base wide rather than concentrated at the front, which is why the next two charts go looking for a cause outside the cohorts themselves.

2 It is not that thin months are poisoning the base. Each frame is one starting month: how many customers arrived, against the share of the base gone a month later. Watch it build and the recent months land inside the same cloud as the early ones. Nothing about the relationship moved across the two years.

What this means

Two things to say here, and the second matters more. A quiet pipeline is not costing you customers elsewhere: the fear that thin months distract the team into losing accounts is not supported at any point in the two years. And the pipeline has not in fact been quiet. New customers have run 52, 43, 51 and 40 over the last four full months against a two year average near 46, so the intake is flat. The sales record reads lower than that only because it is filled in by hand and has fallen behind the billing file.

So acquisition and retention can be run as separate problems by separate owners, and neither the level nor the variation in arrivals explains what churn has done.

3 And it is not hiding at a longer horizon. Give churn one month to happen or six and the cloud lifts without tilting. However long you wait, months with fewer arrivals do not churn more.

What this means

The same answer at every horizon means this is settled rather than merely unproven. Waiting longer for the effect to appear will not help, so nothing further should be spent looking for it.

Where that leaves it

None of the usual explanations survives. Cohorts do not decay faster than they used to, thin months do not poison the base, and nothing appears at a longer horizon. What has moved is on the other side of the ledger: what a customer costs to win has risen far faster than what one returns, and acquisition spend is not available to cut. That leaves price, which is the one lever with room in it and the subject of the rest of this page.

What a single price leaves on the table

-0.50

The anchor is already done. The floor is the open question.

New conversations already open at $2,500, so the half of this that needed testing has been tested. What has not moved is the bottom of the range, and that is where the money is now. Every prospect who will not pay the floor but would have paid something above break-even is a deal turned down after the cost of finding them has already been spent. Acquisition cost is sunk by the time a price is being negotiated: the same $5,217 was spent whether the deal closes at $2,500, at $800, or not at all.

Break-even is about $680 a month at today's cost per logo. A floor at $1,250 turns away everyone between those two numbers. On the fitted demand curve that is roughly nine prospects a month and about $9,300 of new MRR given up, none of which needed a single extra dollar of acquisition spend to reach.

Why the floor is a capacity valve, not a price

There is one good reason to hold a high floor: onboarding can only absorb so many customers, and taking more than it can carry is how a cohort gets a bad first ninety days, which chart 6 shows is where retention is won or lost. That makes the floor a throttle on volume rather than a judgement about which customers are worth having. When onboarding is the binding constraint, raise it. When it is not, lower it toward break-even and take the deals.

Onboarding is not currently the constraint, so the floor should come down.

What has to be true, and what we controlled for

  • Break-even moves with acquisition cost. $680 is computed at $5,217 a logo, the trailing twelve month figure. Cost per logo has run between $2,231 and $7,000 a month across the window, and break-even moves with it, so the floor is better set as a return on cost than as a fixed dollar figure.
  • Capacity is asserted, not measured. That onboarding can absorb more customers is a judgement from the team that does it, not something this data can confirm. If it turns out to be wrong the cost lands in chart 6, as a worse first ninety days for the cohorts taken on at the lower floor.
  • The demand curve is fitted. How many extra prospects a lower floor actually wins is the curve talking, not something observed. The direction is safe; the nine a month is not.

The recommendation

Keep the $2,500 anchor and drop the floor to about $800. That is roughly 1.2 times what a logo costs to win, which leaves room for a bad month on acquisition cost without taking deals that lose money. Below about $700 the margin is too thin to be worth the onboarding slot at today's cost.

Set the floor as a return, not a number. The rule that survives a change in acquisition cost is: take anything returning about 1.2 times cost per logo over its first year. At $5,217 that is $800 a month. If cost per logo climbs back to $7,000 the floor should climb with it, to roughly $1,050, automatically and without a meeting.

Raise it when onboarding becomes the constraint, and not before. The floor is the throttle. The signal to pull it up is the first ninety day retention on chart 6 deteriorating for the newest cohorts, not a view about whether cheap customers are worth having. Review it monthly against that chart and against cost per logo.

What this does not change: the anchor. Nothing here argues for opening lower. The point of a wide range is that the top of it is still collected from customers who will pay it, and the bottom is no longer left unserved.

What is solid and what is not

The logo counts, the flows and the retention curves come straight from the workbook and hold whatever anyone decides about cost allocation. The ratios do not. LTV:CAC and payback both move further on the Customer Success split than on every other correction combined, and that split is an open question rather than a measurement.

One limit worth carrying: gross profit here is what a cohort has actually returned to date rather than a projection, so young cohorts are understated by construction.

Departures are counted two ways, and this page uses one of them on purpose. The push books a churn when it sees the transition; a customer whose subscription drops out of the Stripe export simply stops appearing and never generates one. Counting everyone who stops appearing finds about two thirds more. That is not an open question between two defensible numbers, because only one of them reconciles: over 2026 the booked count gives 311 out against 353 in, which would be a base growing by 42, while the base actually fell from 1,160 to 1,048. Counting everyone who stops appearing gives 493 out against 381 in, a fall of 112, which is exactly what happened. So the larger count is the rate and the booked one is a floor, and every churn figure on this page is the larger count unless it says otherwise.
